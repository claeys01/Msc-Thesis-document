\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap: conclusion}

This work set out to lower the computational cost of turbulent CFD simulations by replacing the most expensive part of the solver loop, the time integration of the discretised Navier--Stokes equations, with a less costly latent integration. The central research question asked how effectively a Neural ODE can accelerate this step without compromising accuracy. To answer it, a hybrid framework was built around the WaterLily solver: a convolutional autoencoder compresses the augmented flow state $(\mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\mu}_0^\epsilon)$ into a latent vector, a Neural ODE $f_\phi$ advances that state in time, and a KNN out-of-distribution monitor (\autoref{sect: KNN}) decides at every step whether the latent rollout can be trusted or whether control is handed back to the conventional solver. The framework was developed and tested on two-dimensional flow past a circular cylinder at $Re=2500$.

At the base operating point, on a $256^2$ grid with a latent dimension $N_z=16$ and multiple-shooting group $n_s=15$. The hybrid loop behaved as intended: it interleaved cheap latent rollouts with conventional CFD steps, with the KNN monitor accepting a rollout until drift was detected past its reference threshold, at which point WaterLily resumed to stabilise the flow. This produced an inference-loop speedup of $S_\text{loop}=1.48$ (\autoref{subsect: 4_wall_time}), confirming that, once trained, the latent rollout integrates the flow more cheaply per step than the reference solver. The same base case also exposed the central limitation of the workflow: once the one-off cost of training the networks was charged to the comparison, the effective speedup fell to $S_\text{eff}=0.07$, an order of magnitude slower than simply running WaterLily, because that training cost was never repaid within a single short simulation window. The disparity between the two metrics is discussed in detail in \autoref{sect: disc_metrics}.

All the parameter studies pointed to the same underlying lever. Narrowing the latent bottleneck, shortening the autoencoder training schedule, or tightening the multiple-shooting group each simplified the dynamics the Neural ODE had to learn, thereby lengthening the average rollout and raising $S_\text{loop}$, but at the cost of coarsening the reconstructed turbulence statistics. Speed and physical fidelity are therefore traded against one another. Various methods influence this trade, either directly through a stricter rollout monitor, which gates how far a rollout is allowed to drift before control is handed back, or indirectly through the autoencoder and the latent dimension, which set how much turbulent detail the compression retains and therefore how long $f_\phi$ can stay in distribution before being flagged. 

The unfavourable base-case economics showed that the performance of $\mathcal{P}$ is tied to the operating point rather than the method itself. Lowering the Reynolds number to $Re=250$ produced a smoother, lower-dimensional wake on which $f_\phi$ was able to sustain longer rollouts before the monitor flagged drift (\autoref{subsect: 4_re250}). Refining the grid to $512^2$ acted through a different mechanism (\autoref{subsect: 4_finer}): because WaterLily is memory-bound while the latent rollout cost is independent of the grid, each replaced step is worth more at higher resolution: refining to $512^2$ lifted $S_\text{eff}$ to $0.25$, and to $0.62$ once the redundant autoencoder epochs were trimmed, narrowing the gap to break-even (\autoref{subsect: 4_finer}). One caveat applies to these figures: every component except the autoencoder training ran on the CPU, so the reported speedups would need to be re-established before being transferred to a GPU setting (\autoref{sect: disc_hardware}).



\section{Answering the Research Question}
\label{sect: conclusion_rq}

\noindent\textbf{How effectively can neural ordinary differential equations accelerate the time integration step in CFD solvers for unsteady flow simulations without compromising accuracy?}

The central research question can be answered from two perspectives. The developed framework behaves differently depending on the time window used to assess its performance. When assessing the neural ODE rollout per step, the answer is a clear yes: once $\mathcal{P}$ is trained, the latent rollout integrates the flow more cheaply than the reference solver. The accuracy of the time integration is maintained not by the Neural ODE being uniformly accurate, but by the KNN monitor verifying if the rollout is not drifting away from the reference set. In that sense, the framework accelerates time integration \emph{and} maintains controllable accuracy, which is the result the research question sought.

Assessing the total cost of the framework is conditional and provides a more honest view of the acceleration scheme. At the $256^2$ base case, the effective speedup was $S_\text{eff}=0.07$, an order of magnitude slower than the reference solver once the network training was charged in the hybrid wall time. This gap is not a property of the developed method but of the operating point: because the rollout cost is fixed while a WaterLily step becomes more expensive with grid size, refining the grid size could potentially lead to an effective speedup towards a break-even point (\autoref{subsect: 4_RE5000}). The Neural ODE, coupled with the autoencoder, is therefore an effective accelerator, particularly in regimes where a conventional CFD step is sufficiently costly to justify learning to replace it.

\subsection{Physical accuracy across regimes, Reynolds numbers and geometries}


\noindent\textbf{How well does the neural ODE acceleration framework preserve the flow’s statistical observables when transferred across Reynolds numbers and grid resolution?}

% Across the tested regimes, accuracy was never compromised silently, as the monitor returned control to the solver whenever a rollout left the validated region. Transferring the architecture from the chaotic $Re=2500$ wake to the smoother $Re=250$ regime (\autoref{subsect: 4_re250}) and to the finer $512^2$ grid was executed with minimal loss of fidelity. The geometric axis, however, was not tested: every result was obtained on the circular cylinder, so accuracy holds across the Reynolds numbers and resolutions studied, but generalisation across geometry remains open and is left to future work. But transferring to a different geometry should be feasible with the current architecture, since this was kept in mind during development and is easily achievable using the BDIM method WaterLily employs. 

\subsection{Sensitivity to architecture and training data quality}

\noindent\textbf{How sensitive is the solution accuracy of neural ODEs to network architecture and training data quality?}

The method is bound by a plethora of parameters that influence its accuracy and behaviour, so a dedicated sensitivity study is a logical next step; current knowledge is limited to one-at-a-time sweeps around a single baseline. What those sweeps did make clear is that what matters most is how well the current simulation state is represented in the training trajectories $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_z$. Since $f_\phi$ and the KNN monitor both draw their logic from $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_z$, the rollout runs long when the active regime overlaps the training distribution and is flagged almost immediately when it does not. Rollout length is therefore a direct reflection of that overlap, and accuracy depends less on raw data volume than on whether the data covers the state the simulation currently occupies.

With respect to sensitivity to architecture, the framework is most sensitive to whatever determines the complexity of the latent dynamics $f_\phi$ must learn. For example, the latent dimension size $N_z$. A narrower bottleneck simplifies those dynamics and allows for longer rollouts, but discards wake detail, thereby polluting the reconstructed statistics, whereas a wider bottleneck recovers fidelity at the cost of acceleration. Architecture, therefore, does not have a single optimum but sets the position on the speed--fidelity trade, which is why a joint sweep is needed to locate it for a given accuracy target.

\section{Recommendations and Future Work}
\label{sect: conclusion_future}

The methods developed in this work provide an interesting basis for future work; the complex parameter landscape leaves a wide range of interactions that would benefit the acceleration performance of $\mathcal{P}$.

\subsection{Training set and current simulation state correlation}
The framework's retraining functionality is useful only when the current simulation state is well represented in the data used for retraining. When the simulation drifted into an uncovered regime, the new snapshots entered as a minority, the loss stayed dominated by the older dynamics, and the update barely shifted the fit. The number of new data points sampled should therefore not be fixed, but a function of the distributional distance between the current state and the training set, so that the networks receive a relevant update. In addition to flexible sampling, a study into whether to retrain on the augmented set or on the new trajectory alone could be relevant for further research. 


\subsection{Parameter Sensitivity analysis}
The parameter studies were one-at-a-time sweeps with a single run per configuration. This exposed the dominant lever, but it cannot locate an optimum, especially since a single run on a chaotic wake carries large variance depending on where the monitor first flags. A joint sweep with repeated runs is recommended, which would capture the parameter interactions a one-at-a-time study misses and the genuine optimum of the speed--fidelity trade for a given accuracy target.


\subsection{Transfer to 3D simulation}
Since the unfavourable economics are a property of the operating point rather than the method, three-dimensional flow is the most promising extension: the per-step cost is far higher and the available compression ratio larger, so the margin that already widened from $256^2$ to $512^2$ should widen further. The catch is that a volumetric autoencoder increases the training cost, which already keeps $S_\text{eff}$ below unity. Whether the larger per-step advantage repays that heavier training, ideally on a GPU backend (\autoref{sect: disc_hardware}), could be an interesting subject of further research.

\subsection{Geometric and Reynolds-number generalisation}
Every result here was obtained on the cylinder at a single Reynolds number per run, so accuracy across asymmetric and irregular geometries could further demonstrate the framework's generalisability. Conditioning the networks on the Reynolds number, so that one pipeline spans several regimes rather than a single one, would further amortise the training cost that dominates the workflow and move the method from a case-specific accelerator to a more general one.
